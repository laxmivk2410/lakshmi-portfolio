Overview

What this project is about

Skye Air Mobility operates drone fleets for logistics and delivery. Their internal operations team needed a platform to track every drone — where it is, what it's doing, whether it's healthy, and who's responsible for it.

When I joined, the team was managing this with a mix of spreadsheets, manual check-ins, and an early version of an internal tool that had grown too complicated to use properly. My job was to redesign the experience from the ground up.

"The old system made our team feel like they were fighting the software instead of managing the fleet. We needed something that felt like it was built for us — not adapted from something else."

The Problem

What wasn't working

Before starting any design work, I spent time with the operations team to understand what they were actually dealing with every day.

😵
Too many screens to check

Users had to jump between 4–5 different views to get a complete picture of fleet status. Critical information was scattered.

⏰
Slow to find what matters

During active operations, users needed information fast. The old layout buried important data under layers of navigation.

👥
Different roles, same view

Admins, operators, and managers all used the same interface — but each had very different needs and priorities.

My Process

How I approached it

1
Research — talking to the people who actually use it

I spent the first two weeks sitting with operations team members, watching how they used the existing system, and asking what made their job harder than it needed to be. I ran structured interviews with 6 team members across 3 different roles.

2
Define — mapping user types and their core needs

I created user personas for each role — Operator, Admin, and Manager — and mapped their daily workflows. Each role had different information needs and different definitions of "urgency".

3
Ideate — user flows and information architecture

In FigJam, I mapped out how information should be structured across the platform. The key question was: what does each user need to see the moment they open the app, without clicking anything?

4
Design — wireframes to high-fidelity

Started with low-fidelity wireframes to test layout logic, then moved to high-fidelity in Figma. Built a design system alongside to keep everything consistent across all platform screens.

5
Test and iterate — usability sessions with real users

Ran usability testing sessions with 4 operations team members. Identified 3 key navigation issues and redesigned those sections before handoff to engineering.

Research Insights

What I found out

Six interviews, two weeks of observation, and a lot of notes. These were the four things that changed how I approached the design:

"I check the same three things every 10 minutes. I just want them on one screen."

— Drone Operator

"When something goes wrong, I don't have time to look for the information. It needs to find me."

— Operations Lead

"I manage 3 different people. I need to see what everyone is doing without calling them."

— Fleet Manager

"The alerts are supposed to help but there are so many that I've started ignoring them."

— Senior Operator

Design Decisions

Key things I changed and why

1. One dashboard, not five. The most important finding was that users were spending most of their time moving between screens. I consolidated fleet status, alerts, and active operations into a single view. Less navigation means less time wasted when it matters most.

2. Role-based views. Instead of one interface for everyone, I designed three different entry points — one for each user role. Each view shows only what that role needs, in the order they need it.

3. Smart alert prioritisation. Instead of showing every alert equally, I introduced a priority system — critical, warning, and informational. Critical alerts are visually unmissable. Informational alerts are collapsed by default.

4. Design system first. Before designing any screens, I built a component library in Figma — buttons, data tables, status indicators, cards. This meant every new screen we designed was consistent without starting from scratch.

What I Learned

Honest takeaways

💡
Enterprise users are experts

They use the same software for 8+ hours a day. Designing for discoverability matters less than designing for speed and efficiency. They don't need to be guided — they need to be fast.

🔄
Build the system, not just the screens

The design system I built saved weeks of time on later features. Starting there instead of jumping straight to screens was one of the best decisions I made on this project.

👂
The best research happens in context

Watching someone use the old system for 20 minutes told me more than any survey could. Seeing where they hesitated, where they clicked wrong — that's where the real design problems live.

🚧
Constraints make you more creative

Working within technical limitations pushed me to find simpler solutions. Some of my best design decisions came from being told "we can't do that" and figuring out a better way.
